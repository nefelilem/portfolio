Onaka · Product Design · MA Project · Royal College of Art · 2024–2025

Tracking gut health without the anxiety it causes.

A pocketable IoT device and companion app that lets people with chronic GI conditions log symptoms in seconds — without screens, overwhelm, or medicalised interfaces.

UX Research Interaction Design IoT UI Design Data Design Branding
§ 01 Problem where it all began

1 in 5 people in the UK have a GI condition. The tools meant to help make things worse.

Existing apps demand frequent logging, display overwhelming graphs, and create hyper-awareness that worsens anxiety. The gut–brain axis means stress and GI symptoms feed each other — a tool that adds cognitive load actively harms the people it's meant to help.

"Existing solutions need too much effort — they cause more stress than relief."
— Patient interview · 2024

§ 02 Research

Talking to doctors and patients before touching a sketch.

A · Clinicians

Gastroenterologist interviews

Two sessions — project start and mid-development. Key finding: patients can't accurately describe symptoms to doctors without a tracking tool.

B · Patients

Journey mapping

Guided conversations and a day-mapping exercise. Identified the anxiety–symptom cycle and the emotional cost of existing tools.

C · Competitors

App case study

5 users, 5 days. Participants used three leading GI apps and kept diaries. All created hyper-awareness and data overwhelm rather than genuine understanding.

"People need a fast, low-stress way to capture lived experience — and return only the most helpful insights."
— Design insight · synthesised from research
Key insights Anxiety worsens GI symptoms — and vice versa Patients can't recall symptoms without logging Data overload causes hyper-awareness Existing apps feel like another chore Should feel like a companion, not a medical tool
§ 03 How it works

The full loop — in four steps.

Before the decisions that shaped each piece — here's the system at a glance. A pocketable silicone device with three concentric ring buttons, paired with a companion app that surfaces meaning at the end of each day.

Log symptoms instantly

Simple button presses capture pain, anxiety, and bowel movements in real time — without interrupting the moment.

Respond, not just record

When anxiety is logged, the device provides immediate breathing feedback — helping users regulate, not just observe.

Reflect, not overthink

At the end of the day, a single gentle question encourages reflection — without constant tracking or pressure.

Insights, not data overload

Patterns are translated into clear, digestible insights — avoiding complex graphs and medical jargon.

§ 04 Decision 01 the hardest pivot

Why I abandoned gesture input.

Users loved the tactile quality of squeezing fabric — but they couldn't trust their inputs were registering. The data corrupted itself.

Original concept

Squeeze, twist, pinch.

Three gestures to log three symptoms. Tactile and playful — but unreadable to the device.

  • No confirmation that a gesture had registered
  • Squeezing felt satisfying as a fidget — corrupting data
  • FSR sensor couldn't reliably distinguish patterns
  • Robotics expert confirmed: unreliable in advanced systems
Fabric squeeze prototypes — green, pink, and striped variants tested for grip and gesture recognition.
Final solution

Three buttons, integrated into the form.

Buttons give immediate, unambiguous confirmation. Concentric ring layout means each is distinguishable by touch — usable from a pocket without looking.

  • Short / long press differentiates severity
  • Raised ring edges guide fingers without looking
  • NeoPixel confirms input — blue breathing pulse for anxiety
  • No accidental logging during ordinary handling
MDF prototypes — testing edge profiles and raised, distinguishable button shapes for blind operation.
§ 05 Decision 02

Three symptoms — no more.

A second gastroenterologist interview shaped the entire device architecture — asking which daily GI factors matter most to capture.

Design principle

"Capture the minimum data that allows meaningful questions to be asked at end of day."

Pain, anxiety, and bowel movements were chosen because they are spontaneous, identifiable in the moment, and the visible surface of deeper patterns — allowing the app to generate targeted reflective questions rather than asking users to fill in a form.

Pain

Pain — centre button

The most urgent symptom. Short press = mild, long press = severe.

Anx

Anxiety — middle ring

Triggers a blue NeoPixel breathing pulse — 5s inhale / 5s exhale — as immediate calming feedback.

Bowel

Bowel movement — outer ring

Three press durations differentiate between normal, loose, and hard movements.

§ 06 Decision 03

Testing four input methods before writing a line of app code.

Four Figma prototypes — dropdown lists, icon-based input, free text, and voice commands — were tested with users over several days before any development began. The winner defined the app's entire interaction model.

What this meant

Structured multiple-choice prompts only — no blank fields. The app never asks for free-form input, keeping cognitive load lowest at the user's most tired moment.

App principle

The app is for reflection — the device handles all input. Voice was used for AI-generated question phrasing, never for user input.

Colour palette

Soft blues for calm and trust. Red, yellow, and green as symptom identifiers — warm and intuitive, not clinical.

Logo origin

The mark grew from the device form — three concentric rings. The name onaka (お腹) means "tummy" in Japanese — found by searching for words with an "o" that the ring logo could replace.

Logo iteration grid — Journal p.98

Typography & tone

Rounded UI typeface to match the device's organic form. Copy is first-person, non-clinical — the app speaks like a calm friend.
